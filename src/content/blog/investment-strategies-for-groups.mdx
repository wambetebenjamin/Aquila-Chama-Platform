Most chamas start with one investment: lending to members. It works, it is
familiar, and it builds the habit of contributing. But once a group has been
running for two or three years, member loans alone stop being the best use of
the money. Here is how to think about the next step.

## Start with the money you cannot risk

Before any investment conversation, separate the pot into three bands:

- **Liquid (about 30 per cent).** Must be available within a week. A member can
  legitimately ask for their savings back, and the group must be able to pay.
- **Lending (about 50 per cent).** Member loans at your agreed interest rate.
  This is the engine of the group.
- **Invested (about 20 per cent).** Longer-term positions the group can leave
  alone for a year or more.

Groups that ignore the liquid band eventually face a crisis that no investment
return can fix.

## Options worth considering

### Member loans

Return is your interest rate, typically eight to twelve per cent. Risk is
concentrated in people you know, which is both the strength and the weakness.
Keep the term short and the guarantors real.

### Money market funds

Regulated by the Capital Markets Authority, liquid within a few days, and
historically competitive with fixed deposits. Good home for the liquid band,
because the money earns something while it waits.

### Treasury bills and bonds

Safe, predictable, and accessible to groups through a licensed broker. The
catch is the lock-up period, which is why this belongs in the invested band
only.

### Group enterprises

Buying a plot, a water tank, a tent business, a posho mill. The returns can be
excellent and the risks are operational rather than financial. The discipline
here is to write down who runs it, how often they report, and what happens if
it loses money for two quarters running.

> Never invest the group's money in something a member cannot explain to the
> other members in two minutes.

## Questions to answer before any investment

1. Can we explain it to every member in plain language?
2. What is the worst realistic outcome, and can the group survive it?
3. How quickly can we get the money back if three members withdraw?
4. Is the person running it accountable, and to whom?
5. Is it recorded in the minutes?

## Record the return like you record the contribution

An investment that is not tracked is a donation. Put it on the same dashboard as
every contribution and loan, review it at every meeting, and report it in the
annual statement alongside dividends. The groups that do this are the groups
that are still meeting in ten years.
